The Ondes Martinot is an early electronic instrument that I mostly know from the music of Olivier Messiaen. It has a distinctive sound that's closer to a pure sine wave than any traditional acoustic instrument, except for maybe a tuning fork. However, there's a degree of distortion to the sound that makes it difficult to replicate. The Ondes was only manufactured by Maurice Martinot and one successor, making original instruments rare. Due to their use of by todays's standards, crude electronics, they often break down, and they remain niche enough that recreations/replications are almost nonexistant.

The original instruments use vacuum tubes (triodes) to achieve what we would use transistors for today: oscillation, mixing, amplifiers, etc. I wanted to see how close I could get to the original sound of the Ondes, using the orignal instrument's operating principles for signal generation, but using today's solid state electronics instead. 

The electronics and circuits used by the original instrument are very similar to those found in ham radios and other RF equipment. This is unsurprising, considering that Maurice Martinot was a radio operator during the first world war, and was inspired to create the Ondes by the sounds he heard using them. 

There are two types of approximately-sine wave oscillators that were appropriate for this task. I tried both

The absolute capacitance of the air dielectric variable capacitors is very small; a common type used on old AM radios and ham radios today ranges from ~25 to 325-450 pF, which is kind I chose for the first test. It is common in Clapp designs for the variable capacitor to the one placed in series with the inductor, as well as to be very small in comparison to the other two caps. These capacitors go through their whole range in half a turn, so ideally, having the whole capacitance range be roughly correlated to the desired range of pitches is desirable, in order to avoid the latter being only a small sliver of the physical turning action.

With the capacitance range set, and the precise values of the other capacitors negligible, the only variable left to tune is the inductance. The relation between capacitance, inductance and frequency resulted in a balancing act between having the inductor be large enough to enable the ~75000-80000 frequency range be reachable, but small enough to make the pitch range be as encompassing as the variable capacitance range as possible.

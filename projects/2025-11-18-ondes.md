The Ondes Martinot is an early electronic instrument that I mostly know from the music of Olivier Messiaen. It has a distinctive sound that's closer to a pure sine wave than any traditional acoustic instrument, except for maybe a tuning fork. However, there's a degree of distortion to the sound that makes it difficult to replicate. The Ondes was only manufactured by Maurice Martinot and one successor, making original instruments rare. Due to their use of by todays's standards, crude electronics, they often break down, and they remain niche enough that recreations/replications are almost nonexistant.

The original instruments use vacuum tubes (triodes) to achieve what we would use transistors for today: oscillation, mixing, amplifiers, etc. I wanted to see how close I could get to the original sound of the Ondes, using the orignal instrument's operating principles for signal generation, but using today's solid state electronics instead. 

The electronics and circuits used by the original instrument are very similar to those found in ham radios and other RF equipment. This is unsurprising, considering that Maurice Martinot was a radio operator during the First World War, and was inspired to create the Ondes by the sounds he heard using them. 

The operating principle of the Ondes is very similar to another (and much more well-known) early electronic instrument, the theremin. The outputs of oscillators are mixed (multiplied) together. and the resulting signal contains components with frequencies of the original two signals, the sum of the frequencies, and their difference. Some filtering circuitry removes almost everything except the difference component, and that is amplified and sent to a speaker. By fixing the frequency of one oscillator and varying the frequency of the other, with both at relatively high frequencies, we can create a low frequency signal without needing to build an oscillator for that low frequency, which would have been difficult in the days of limited capacitor sizes and component stability. The range of sounds we want to be able to generate a little bigger than the range of a piano, about 20hz to 5kHz, and the fixed oscillator generates a signal of about ~80 kHz. 

There are two types of sine wave oscillators that were appropriate for this task. I tried both: the Wien-Bridge and the Clapp. In both cases, a variable capacitor is used to change the frequency of the VFO. The Clapp is less finicky to work with and has a much easier time starting up when voltage is applied to the circuit. The Wien-Bridge has the advantage of not needing inductors, creating cleaner sine waves, and using smaller variable capacitance values. I decided to try both.

The two oscillating signals are added together using a summing amplifier built with an op-amp. "But wait", you just thought. "You said that the oscillators are mixed together, not summed!" And indeed, a quick sketch on Desmos shows that summing looks quite different from mixing. There are no new frequency components with the summed signal... yet. If you were to listen to this signal, there would be a "beating" effect, which you can see visually in the graph. The beating is at the frequency of the difference, and you could see how with application of an envelope detector, the resulting signal will be the desired oscillating difference signal. You might also notice that this looks like extracting the signal out of an AM radio signal, which is done with envelope detectors [^1].

Mathematically, the use of the an envelope detector creates the sum and difference components. The core of a envelope detector is a diode, or a triode acting as a diode. This non-linear operation applied by the diode is like squaring the input sum-of-oscillators signal, which creates terms with the frequency difference and sum components; try expanding (cos(at) + cos(bt))^2 and applying the identity cos(x)cos(y) = 0.5(cos(x - y) + cos(x + y)). 

I first designed the circuit, simulating and iterating in LTspice. First, I designed a Clapp oscillator. The absolute capacitance of the air dielectric variable capacitors is very small; a common type used on old AM radios and ham radios today ranges from ~25 to 325-450 pF, which is kind I chose for the first test. It is common in Clapp designs for the variable capacitor to the one placed in series with the inductor, as well as to be very small in comparison to the other two caps. These capacitors go through their whole range in half a turn, so ideally, having the whole capacitance range be roughly correlated to the desired range of pitches is desirable, in order to avoid the latter being only a small sliver of the physical turning action.

With the capacitance range set, and the precise values of the other capacitors negligible, the only variable left to tune is the inductance. The relation between capacitance, inductance and frequency resulted in a balancing act between having the inductor be large enough to enable the ~75000-80000 frequency range be reachable, but small enough to make the pitch range be as encompassing as the variable capacitance range as possible.
